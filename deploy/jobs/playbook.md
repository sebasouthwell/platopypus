# Job Hunt playbook

Treat each job the person pursues as a small project with five stages. Each stage has a checklist (shown to the
person in the app) and work an agent can do. Use what you learn in one stage in the next.

## 1. Research the employer

- **What they do and for whom:** products, services, clients, how they make money. The person should be able to
  explain the company's purpose in a sentence or two.
- **Mission and values:** from the company's own About, careers and values pages. For each value, find one true
  example from Me that shows it. Interviewers listen for the value words, and for real examples behind them.
- **Recent news and health:** announcements, results, funding, layoffs, acquisitions, awards, major contracts in
  the last 12 months, and what it means for this team.
- **People and structure:** who leads the company and the department; who the hiring manager might be, from public
  professional profiles. Never look into private lives.
- **Market:** main competitors, industry trends and challenges. It gives good questions to ask.
- **What working there is like:** employee reviews (look for patterns, not single reviews), the interview process
  others describe, benefits, hybrid or office rules, and training or graduate schemes.
- **Jobs in other countries:** the right to work there for this person's nationality (in the EU: free movement for EU
  citizens; otherwise the EU Blue Card or the country's national permits, their salary thresholds, and recognition of
  qualifications), the language the job really needs, and local application customs (CV format, photo, cover-letter
  length, references). Use official government sources and say which country's rules you used.
- **The right to work:** whether the person needs sponsorship. In the UK, check the Home Office Register of Licensed
  Sponsors (GOV.UK) for the employer **and the right route** (Skilled Worker, not only temporary routes). Say when
  the register was last updated and that a listing is a point-in-time check.
- Keep every source with its link, and end with questions the person could ask the interviewer, based on what you found.

## 2. Tailor

- Pull the requirements out of the advert, essential and desirable ones apart. Map each to evidence in Me.
- **CV:** use the advert's own words for skills and titles where they are true (applicant tracking systems match
  words; people skim). Lead each bullet with what the person did and what came of it, with numbers where Me has them.
  Keep the layout simple: standard headings, no text in images or tables for ATS-heavy employers. Put the most
  relevant section first. Suggest section variants; don't rewrite the whole CV.
- **Cover letter:** about one A4 page in UK style, three or four paragraphs. Address a named person if one is
  public. Paragraph 1: the role and why this company, with one specific reason from the research (a value, a product,
  recent news). Paragraphs 2–3: two or three requirements, each backed by a short STAR example from Me. Close: what
  they would bring, and that they look forward to discussing it. No clichés, nothing untrue.
- **Application questions** ("why us", "tell us about a time…"): draft answers in STAR form within the word limit.

## 3. Apply

- Check the deadline and when the advert closes early. Note which CV version and letter were sent, any reference
  number, and the date. Follow the employer's instructions exactly (file type, naming, portal).
- Online tests are common for graduate roles (numerical, verbal, situational judgement, coding). Name the likely
  tests and where to practise.

## 4. Prepare for interview

- **Competency questions:** prepare 6–8 STAR stories from Me (Situation, Task, Action, Result) that between them
  cover the job's competencies, with a different story for each competency where possible. Most of each answer is the
  Action, told in the first person ("I", not "we"), and the Result says what changed, with numbers where possible.
- Likely questions: motivation ("why this role, why us"), the competencies in the advert, technical questions for the
  field, strengths and weaknesses, a mistake or failure, teamwork and conflict, and scenario questions ("what would you
  do if…") at assessment centres.
- Assessment centres: group exercises (listen, include others, keep to time), presentations, case studies and
  in-tray exercises.
- Questions to ask them: about the team, how success is measured in the first months, training, and something
  from the research. Avoid asking anything the website already answers.
- Logistics: format, who is interviewing, timing, and what to bring. Practise, but don't learn answers word for word.

## 5. Follow up

- A short thank-you message within a day, mentioning something specific from the conversation.
- If rejected, ask for feedback and add what is learned to Me's gaps. If offered, compare the offer: salary, take-home
  and living costs where the job is, pension, holiday, hybrid working, training, progression, and the start date.
  Negotiate on facts (market pay, competing offers), politely.

## Always

- Everything you write says only true things from Me. When something is missing, ask or record a gap.
- Never contact an employer, apply, or send anything. Prepare it, and the person sends it.
- Never put Me, the CV or personal details into web searches. Search for the employer and the role.
